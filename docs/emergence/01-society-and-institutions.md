# Society, Households, Institutions, and Polities

## Purpose

Create a social substrate from which villages, guilds, companies, religions, militias, political groups, criminal organizations, and eventually states emerge.

> Organizations are persistent coordination patterns among agents, not arbitrary labels.

## Households

A household is the smallest persistent economic/social unit above an individual. Track members, dependents, residence, property, liquid wealth, stored goods, food stores, debts, contracts, income sources, production capabilities, reputation, goals, and succession policy.

Households form through birth, partnership, adoption, migration, cohabitation, and economic necessity. They split when children become independent, property is divided, conflict persists, opportunities separate members, or migration occurs.

## Relationships

Typed relationships include family, friend, employer, employee, customer, supplier, debtor, creditor, rival, mentor, apprentice, leader, follower, ally, opponent, witness, and enemy.

Track trust, affection, fear, respect, obligation, resentment, reputation, recency, and strength. Relationships decay or transform unless reinforced.

## Institutions

An institution is a persistent organization with membership, shared purpose, leadership, resources, assets, rules, reputation, obligations, external relationships, territory/influence, and historical memory.

Examples: guild, militia, merchant company, religious order, cooperative, school, criminal group, council, labor organization, hunting lodge.

## Emergence

Institution candidates arise when clusters repeatedly coordinate around a common problem or opportunity. Candidate scoring considers participant count, interaction frequency, shared goals, trust, resource pooling, repeated success, common identity, leadership, and external pressure.

Use hysteresis: formation, stabilization, and dissolution thresholds prevent flicker.

## Leadership

Leaders emerge from trust, competence, resources, reputation, coercive capacity, social centrality, and historical success. Informal leaders can precede formal leadership.

## Institution goals

Derive goals from member needs, pooled dependencies, leadership priorities, external threats, historical grievances, and institutional identity. Examples include securing iron, protecting farmland, reducing taxes, acquiring markets, suppressing banditry, expanding pilgrimage, or defending sacred sites.

## Institution actions

Recruit, negotiate, buy/sell, contract, build, patrol, petition, strike, boycott, sponsor events, spread information, migrate, ally, compete, sanction, and fight. All actions resolve through deterministic rules.

## Governance

Settlements need coordination once population and interdependence pass thresholds. Governance emerges from resource concentration, trust networks, enforcement capacity, legitimacy, military power, specialization, and historical precedent.

Possible forms include council, hereditary leadership, merchant oligarchy, clan leadership, priesthood, military rule, cooperative administration, and representative governance. Governance must change actual decision pathways, not just display text.

## Legitimacy

Legitimacy derives from protection, food security, justice, tradition, wealth distribution, religious authority, military success, and popular trust. Low legitimacy can produce protests, defections, tax resistance, rival leaders, migration, coups, and civil conflict.

## Crime

Crime emerges when valuable goods, weak enforcement, unmet needs, profitable fencing, and favorable risk/reward coexist. Repeated successful cooperation can produce organized crime institutions.

## Religion

Shared beliefs and repeated rituals can eventually form belief communities. Inputs include extraordinary events, charismatic individuals, natural phenomena, ancestors, bosses, disasters, magic, and sacred locations. Outputs include rituals, clergy, sacred places, taboos, festivals, pilgrimages, schisms, and religious economies.

## Invariants

1. No institution without members.
2. No stable institution without repeated coordination.
3. No authority without legitimacy or coercion.
4. No reputation without observations.
5. No collective action without coordination.
6. Institutions can fail, merge, split, migrate, or disappear.
