# Settlements, Cities, Infrastructure, and Civilization

## Purpose

Settlements emerge from population, resources, infrastructure, trade, safety, and institutions. A city is not a larger prefab; it is a dense network of specialized flows and institutions.

## Settlement state

Track population, households, food, water, housing, employment, production, imports, exports, wealth, security, health, infrastructure, institutions, governance, reputation, and historical importance.

## Carrying capacity

Population capacity depends on food, water, housing, jobs, sanitation, security, transport, trade, climate, and disease. Population should move toward capacity rather than use arbitrary growth.

## Formation and growth

Settlement candidates emerge around water, fertile land, mines, fishing, crossings, ports, sacred sites, roads, defensive locations, and economic hubs. Formation requires enough population, resources, shelter, security, and social coordination.

Growth persists when income, food, safety, opportunity, and connectivity remain favorable. Growth creates demand for housing, roads, markets, workshops, governance, sanitation, and defense.

## Specialization

Infer specialization from persistent production and trade: farming center, mining town, port, fortress, religious center, manufacturing city, academic center, or trading hub. Do not store specialization as a static content tag when it can be derived from flows.

## Infrastructure

Roads, bridges, docks, wells, aqueducts, walls, markets, storage, workshops, and public buildings have capacity, condition, maintenance cost, ownership, dependencies, and historical origin.

Repeated movement upgrades path → trail → road. Repeated crossings can produce ford → ferry → bridge. Trade can produce rest stop → inn → market → settlement.

Infrastructure degrades. Failure increases travel time, reduces trade, causes shortages and migration. Repair restores flows.

## City threshold

Use multiple dimensions rather than population alone: population density, economic specialization, institutional complexity, infrastructure density, trade connectivity, and administrative capacity.

## City metabolism

```text
inputs: food, water, raw materials, labor, information
processes: production, trade, construction, governance
outputs: goods, wealth, waste, knowledge, population change
```

## Decline

Persistent food shortage, disease, war, trade loss, resource exhaustion, ecological collapse, political instability, or infrastructure failure can cause contraction, migration, abandonment, and ruin.

## Dependency networks

Cities depend on external sources. Example:

```text
City A
  grain ← Village B
  iron ← Mine C
  timber ← Forest D
  fish ← Lake E
```

Strategic gameplay therefore concerns flows, not just territory.

## Polities and civilization

Later, settlements can form alliances, leagues, kingdoms, republics, federations, and empires. A polity is a higher-order institution with territory, subjects, resources, laws, and enforcement.

Civilization should initially be an analytical aggregation of shared institutions, technologies, culture, political structures, economic networks, and historical continuity—not a separate hard-coded game layer.
